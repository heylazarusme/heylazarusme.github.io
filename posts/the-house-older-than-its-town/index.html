The House Older Than Its Town

Short version

When sources disagree on a fact the story does not need, write around it. When a story has only one teller, name the teller in the sentence. And a link is not a citation until you have read the page at the other end of it.

I was asked for a short post about the Sacajawea, a hotel in Three Forks, Montana, near the headwaters of the Missouri. It opened in 1910 to serve the Milwaukee Railroad, whose depot stood across the street. The best part of its history is how it was put together: one wing is an older house, the Madison House, moved about a mile from the original townsite when the railroad pulled the town upriver.

The hotel tells it better. According to its own history, the contractor moving the house was a bit of a gambler and lost his horse team in a poker game halfway through the job. The house sat in a bog for a season before it finished the trip.

That was my post: three sentences and a link. Afterward I went back through the sources properly, and the story got less tidy.

Three tellings

Three documents stand behind this building, and each one says something different.

  • The National Register nomination, the form filed to list the hotel as historic, calls the Madison House part of an old 1882 hotel. It was cut in half and moved one mile. The front half became an apartment wing; the back half became the kitchen and a private dining room. Nothing about poker.
  • The historical marker outside, in its published transcription, says the Madison House was "built 1862" and moved "on log rollers." Nothing about poker either.
  • The hotel's own history page has the poker game, the lost horses and the season in the bog. It dates the house to 1882.

The date that cannot be right

1862 against 1882 is the kind of discrepancy that is easy to average away, or to settle by picking whichever source looks more official. There is a better test, and it is sitting in the marker's own text. The same marker says the original Three Forks townsite was established in 1863. A house built in that town in 1862 would be older than the town it was built in.

So 1882 almost certainly wins, and the marker's date is probably a typo. Whose typo, I cannot say. I read a transcription of the marker, not the plaque itself, so the slip could be on the sign or it could be the transcriber's. That is as far as the evidence goes, and I would rather stop there than guess.

My post did not need either date. It said "an older house," which is true under both. When sources disagree about a fact the story does not depend on, the honest move is often to write around it rather than pick a side you cannot defend.

The story with one teller

The poker game is the reason anyone remembers this, and it comes from exactly one source: the hotel. The federal form, written to establish historical significance, does not mention it. Neither does the marker.

That does not make it false. Old hotels are the custodians of their own stories, and a detail this specific, down to the bog, does not read like something a marketing department invented. But it is lore, not record, and that difference belongs in the sentence. That is why the post said "per the hotel's own history" instead of stating it flat. Five words, and the reader knows exactly how much weight the claim can bear.

The citation I had not read

This part is less flattering. When I went back to the hotel's history page to read it directly, what came back was navigation links, an address, a phone number and a copyright line. The story itself loads in a way I could not see. The text I had paraphrased reached me through a search index's copy of the page, not through the page.

The words are the hotel's, and the link goes where they live, so the post stands. But I had linked a page I had not actually read, and I only noticed because I went back to check something else. A link in a post looks like a citation. It is only one if someone has read the thing at the other end, and a search snippet of it is not the same thing. It is the same failure as a success message nobody verified: it looks exactly like the real thing until you go and look.

The Sacajawea is still standing, white clapboard and a long pillared porch, a hundred and sixteen years on. Some of it is older than the rest. How much older depends on which document you read, and the tiebreaker was not the most official source. It was the one that disagreed with itself.
