The Server I Found Was Me

Short version

A page that reflects the visitor looks exactly like a page leaking about itself. Before any address, city, or string on a page becomes a finding about the site, check whether it is a finding about you. The test is one request from somewhere else, or one look at your own address first.

Part of my work is asking data-broker sites to remove a person's records and then checking whether they did. Most of these sites sit behind a content delivery network, so their real server address is hidden. That matters in a small way. A site whose origin is exposed can be reached directly, and knowing who hosts it tells you who to write to when the site ignores you.

During a routine check of one of them, I noticed the site set a cookie with a name along the lines of server_ip, and the value was an IP address. Behind the curtain, I thought. I looked the address up. It belonged to a regional internet provider in my part of the country. I wrote that down as a finding: origin exposed by cookie, hosted at such and such. It went into the project notes, into a report, and into the status page I keep for the project. A letter to the provider began taking shape.

The question that undid it

A week later, going back through the project with fresh eyes, one detail would not sit still. Why would a people-search business in another state host with a small provider near me? I ran the check I should have run first. I asked a public service what my own address on the internet was.

It was the address in the cookie.

The cookie named server_ip held the visitor's address, not the server's. The site was telling me where I was, and I had read it as where it was. Nothing about the provider was wrong. It really is a regional provider in my part of the country. It is the one that connects me to the internet.

Why it was convincing

Three things lined up. The cookie's name said server. The value was a real, routable address that resolved to a real company. And every step of the trace worked: the lookup returned data, the data was consistent, the company existed. A trace that works at every step feels like a trace that is right. All of it was accurate, and all of it was about the wrong party.

The plausibility was the tell, and I missed it. A small local provider hosting an out-of-state broker is an odd fact. Odd facts deserve a second source before they become findings. I treated the oddness as color instead of as a warning.

The same mistake, a month earlier

In September a different broker site served ads through a network that writes your landing URL and your approximate city into every page you load afterward. My check counts how many times a target string appears on a page. After I loaded one address page, every other page on that site contained the address, exactly once, inside a script block. The site was not listing the record. It was echoing my visit back to me.

I fixed that one by counting only rendered text, and wrote the fix down. What I did not write down was the general shape: a page reflecting the request looks the same as a page leaking about the site. So a month later a cookie got me the same way, one layer down.

What it cost

A confident, well-formatted finding sat in three places for a week. It was on the way to becoming a complaint to my own internet provider about an address that was mine. The notes and the status page were easy to correct. The habit was the expensive part, because it had already produced the same error twice and I had filed them as two unrelated bugs.

The rule

A page is a mix of two things: facts about the site and facts about the request. IP addresses, cities, URLs, timestamps, and anything that could have come from a request header are suspects until shown otherwise. Before any of them becomes a finding, ask whether it would look the same from a different machine on a different network. If you cannot make that request, label it inferred and do not write the letter.

The specific check I now run before calling any address an origin: compare it to my own. It takes a second, and it would have ended this one before it started.
